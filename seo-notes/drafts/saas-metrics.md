# SaaS Metrics: What to Track at Each Stage, With 2026 Benchmarks

The three highest-ranking guides for "saas metrics" in our November 2026 check list 5, 14 and 21 metrics. Each gives formulas. None gives a dated benchmark, and none maps metrics to stages. The surveys that do cut by stage disagree, and on growth they disagree most at the smallest size. [High Alpha's 2026 Startup Benchmarks Report](https://www.highalpha.com/saas-benchmarks) puts median ARR growth for companies under $1M ARR at 100%. [SaaS Capital's 2026 growth report](https://www.saas-capital.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/RB34WS1-2026-B2B-Benchmarking-Private-SaaS-Company-Growth-Rates.pdf) says "A growth rate of 30% for a less than $1 million SaaS business is below the median", and its chart reads about 40%. Both are real numbers. They measured different companies.

This page sets the three newest surveys (High Alpha 2026, Benchmarkit 2026 and SaaS Capital 2026) side by side by ARR band, shows where they disagree, and marks when each metric starts to mean something, using the publishers' own caveats where they exist.

The short answer, by ARR band (our reading, grounded in those caveats):

- **Under $1M ARR:** MRR and its growth rate, gross margin, customers lost each month, burn and runway.
- **$1M to $5M:** add CAC payback, net revenue retention once you have a cohort a year old, and the burn multiple.
- **$5M to $20M:** add the magic number, and start treating gross revenue retention and LTV:CAC as reliable.
- **From about $20M:** add the Rule of 40.

Metrics with their own Startup Yeti page get a formula, a 2026 figure and a link. MRR, ARR, growth, gross margin and runway are covered in full here.

## Which SaaS metrics matter at each stage

We key stages to ARR, not to funding rounds. We found no primary source that maps pre-seed, seed, Series A or Series B to an ARR level in 2026.

| Metric | Under $1M ARR | $1M to $5M | $5M to $20M | $20M+ | Basis for the timing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MRR, ARR and growth rate | Core | Core | Core | Core | Our reading: most metrics below are built from it |
| Gross margin | Track | Core | Core | Core | Our reading; Sacks (Craft Ventures, 2021) wants at least 75% in the long term |
| Burn and runway | Core | Core | Core | Core | Our reading: cash can run out at any stage |
| Customer churn | Core | Core | Core | Core | Our reading: countable from the first cancellation |
| Gross revenue retention (GRR) | Early | Early | Core | Core | Benchmarkit's 2025 report: under $5M ARR, GRR may not reflect real churn until after the first or second renewal |
| Net revenue retention (NRR) | Not yet | Early | Core | Core | Our reading: NRR needs a cohort at least 12 months old |
| CAC payback | Understated | Core | Core | Core | High Alpha 2026: sub-$1M figures understated by unallocated founder time and costs |
| LTV:CAC | Not yet | Early | Core | Core | Our reading: LTV divides by churn, which needs history |
| Magic number | Not yet | Early | Core | Core | Our reading: needs sales and marketing spend large enough to measure |
| Burn multiple | Volatile | Core | Core | Core | Sacks (2020): pre-revenue it does not compute; a seed company "might have a Burn Multiple of 3" |
| Rule of 40 | Not yet | Not yet | Near $20M | Core | Benchmarkit 2026: more meaningful near $20M ARR; Feld (2015): companies at $50M+ revenue |

**Core:** track it and report it. **Track:** watch it, but don't benchmark hard yet. **Early:** computable, but young cohorts or small samples make it noisy. **Not yet:** the inputs don't exist or the number misleads. **Understated** and **Volatile:** computable but biased.

## Revenue: MRR, ARR and growth rate

### What counts as MRR and ARR

MRR is the recurring subscription revenue your active customers bring in each month. ARR is MRR × 12. David Skok's [SaaS Metrics 2.0 definitions](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2-definitions-2/) call it annualized run rate: "the annual run-rate of recurring revenue from the current installed base." Bessemer Venture Partners, in [Scaling to $100 Million](https://www.bvp.com/atlas/scaling-to-100-million) (2021), says ARR "nets out non-recurring revenue", so setup fees, consulting and one-off licences stay out. $50,000 of MRR is $600,000 of ARR, and a $20,000 implementation project that month changes neither.

Net new ARR is new ARR plus expansion minus churned ARR (Skok). The magic number and the burn multiple both use it, so get it right once.

Committed ARR (CARR), in Bessemer's usage, adds contracts signed but not yet live. Our reading: report it if your board wants it, but label it, because the surveys on this page report ARR, not CARR. Usage revenue moves with consumption, so one month × 12 can overstate or understate a year (see the FAQ below). Our guide to [usage-based pricing](/marketing/usage-based-pricing) covers the model itself.

### Growth rate and CMGR

**YoY ARR growth = (ARR now - ARR 12 months ago) ÷ ARR 12 months ago.** High Alpha measures it as ARR at the end of Q2 2026 against the end of Q2 2025.

Early on, a monthly view helps. Compound monthly growth rate (CMGR) is (MRR now ÷ MRR n months ago) raised to the power 1 ÷ n, minus 1. Doubling in a year is a CMGR of 5.9%. Growing 40% in a year is 2.8% a month.

David Sacks' [2021 list of Craft's Series A benchmarks](https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-saas-metrics-that-matter) asks for a CMGR of at least 15% below $1M ARR and 10% above, and notes that "A CMGR of 10% is about 3x year-over-year." The arithmetic holds: 1.10 to the 12th power is 3.14. At 15% a month it is 5.35x a year. That is one firm's view in the 2021 market, not a measured norm.

Who was measured, and when:

Respondents gave cash burn as a range, scored at the midpoint, and other answers look bucketed too, so treat medians as bucket values. The report does not publish n per metric.
- **SaaS Capital, 2026 Private B2B SaaS Company Growth Rate Benchmarks** (July 2026): its 15th annual survey, more than 1,000 private B2B SaaS companies including bootstrapped ones; full-year 2025 growth. Overall median 22%, down from 25% in 2024. The labels on the first two bands of its chart are hard to place, so treat about 40% and 25% as approximate.
- **[Benchmarkit 2026 report](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2026-saas-ai-native-metrics)** (June 2026): a self-reported survey of 342 B2B SaaS and AI-native companies, calendar 2025 data. Overall median 20% (8% to 42%, n = 338), down from 30% for 2022. Only 22.8% of participants were under $5M ARR, and the report has no cut below $5M.

Chart-read means we read the value off the report's chart because it isn't printed as a number.

The gap at the small end is the useful part. Our reading: High Alpha's sample is younger. More than half its respondents were founded in 2022 or later, and High Alpha reports that those companies grow about twice as fast as older peers below $1M ARR. SaaS Capital includes bootstrapped companies, whose median across all sizes was 20% against 25% for equity-backed ones. So a 60% grower under $1M ARR is behind High Alpha's median and ahead of SaaS Capital's. Name the survey next to the number.

SaaS Capital also gives a top-quartile line: "A $2 million SaaS company needs to be growing at nearly 50% year-over-year to be in the top 25% of its peers, whereas that bar is 31% growth for a company with $20 million of ARR."

### Rules of thumb: T2D3 and 5 to 7% a week

T2D3, the "3 3 2 2 2 rule", comes from Neeraj Agrawal of Battery Ventures, writing in [TechCrunch in February 2015](https://techcrunch.com/2015/02/01/the-saas-travel-adventure/). It starts after product-market fit and $2M ARR: triple to $6M, triple to $18M, then double to $36M, $72M and $144M. Agrawal wrote, "This is not the only growth path."

Tripling is 200% growth. High Alpha's 75th percentile for $1M to $5M is 145%, and SaaS Capital's top-quartile line for a $2M company is nearly 50%. So T2D3's first year sits above the top quartile in both surveys. A path for the fastest companies, not a median plan.

Paul Graham wrote in ["Startup = Growth"](http://www.paulgraham.com/growth.html) (2012) that a good growth rate during Y Combinator is 5 to 7% a week. Compounded, 5% a week is about 12.6x a year. Graham framed it as a rate for the Y Combinator period; the yearly figure is only our arithmetic.

## Gross margin

**Gross margin = (revenue - cost of revenue) ÷ revenue.** In SaaS, cost of revenue usually covers hosting, the third-party software and data needed to run the product, and frontline support.

Software (subscription) gross margin divides subscription revenue minus its cost by subscription revenue; total gross margin includes services. Benchmarkit's 2026 report shows both: a software gross margin median of 80% (72% to 86%, n = 232) and a total-revenue median of 76% (68% to 84%, n = 228). Low-margin services pull the total down, so we would benchmark the subscription line.

By band, the surveys disagree again. High Alpha puts software gross margin at 80% under $1M ARR and 75% at $1M to $5M. Benchmarkit's under $5M median is 72% (25th percentile 50%, 75th 80%, chart-read). The bands differ, but that is an 8-point gap between the two surveys' smallest buckets.

Guidance, not measurement: Sacks wants at least 75% in the long term, and Bessemer's 2021 guide aims for 65 to 70% over time. For AI products, High Alpha reports a median inference cost of 5% of revenue where AI is core to the product against 2.5% where AI is a supporting feature, with similar gross margins.

Gross margin matters beyond itself because LTV and CAC payback both multiply by it. Take $100 of monthly revenue per account, 2% monthly churn and a $1,000 CAC (illustrative). At 80% margin, LTV is $100 × 0.80 ÷ 0.02 = $4,000 and payback is $1,000 ÷ ($100 × 0.80) = 12.5 months. At 72%, LTV is $3,600 and payback 13.9 months. That 8-point gap cuts LTV by 10% and stretches payback by 11%. A metric computed on revenue instead of gross profit skips this step and flatters you.

## Retention: churn, GRR and NRR

**Customer churn = customers lost in the period ÷ customers at the start.** **Gross revenue churn = MRR lost to cancellations and downgrades ÷ MRR at the start**, and GRR is the annual mirror: 1 minus annual gross revenue churn. Benchmarkit's 2026 report puts median GRR at 84% (76% to 91%, n = 226), which is 16% of the revenue base lost in a year, about 1.44% a month. It has no ARR cut for GRR; High Alpha's 2026 median for $1M to $5M is 89% (80% to 98%). Our [SaaS churn rate guide](/startup/saas-churn-rate) covers the monthly to annual conversion and which basis belongs in a deck.

Read early GRR with care. Benchmarkit's 2025 edition warned that under $5M ARR, GRR "may not reflect the actual customer ARR churn until after the first and/or second renewal periods." High Alpha's sub-$1M median is 100%. Our reading: that is cohorts that haven't reached a renewal yet, not a band with no churn.

**NRR = (starting MRR + expansion - contraction - churn) ÷ starting MRR**, for a fixed cohort, usually over 12 months. New customers stay out. Benchmarkit's 2026 median is 102% (n = 230), and 94% for companies under $5M ARR (chart-read). High Alpha's 2026 figure for $1M to $5M is 109% (92% to 114%): a narrower band, but 15 points above Benchmarkit's under $5M bucket. More in [net revenue retention](/startup/net-revenue-retention).

## Acquisition efficiency: CAC payback, LTV:CAC and magic number

**CAC payback (months) = CAC ÷ (monthly revenue per new customer × gross margin).** Benchmarkit's 2026 median is 16 months (10 to 24, n = 198); that report cuts payback by contract size, not ARR. High Alpha's 2026 report, which defines payback on software gross margin, gives 10 months (8 to 14) at $1M to $5M and 14 (8 to 17) at $5M to $20M. Its sub-$1M median is 2 months, and High Alpha itself says to "Treat the sub-$1M figures cautiously, since unallocated founder time and costs may understate early CAC." The "under 12 months" target traces to Skok in 2011. Contract-size benchmarks and formula variants are in our [CAC payback period guide](/startup/cac-payback-period).

**LTV:CAC = (monthly revenue per customer × gross margin ÷ monthly churn) ÷ CAC.** Benchmarkit's 2026 median is 4.1x (2.7x to 7.8x, n = 146), and 4.6x for companies under $5M ARR (chart-read). The 3:1 rule is Skok's, from 2009, where he called 3x "a rough minimum." So 3:1 is a floor on a gross-margin basis, not a typical result. See [LTV to CAC ratio](/startup/ltv-to-cac-ratio).

**Magic number = (this quarter's recurring revenue - last quarter's) × 4 ÷ last quarter's sales and marketing expense.** On an ARR basis it is net new ARR ÷ last quarter's S&M, with no ×4. Sacks' 2021 definition is the ARR version: "Net New ARR in a period divided by S&M expense from the prior period." One of the top-ranking glossaries we read defines the magic number as lifetime value divided by CAC. That is LTV:CAC under another name, and it answers a different question. Benchmarkit's 2026 median is 1.37 (0.68 to 2.14, n = 132), with no ARR cut. The 0.75 line founders quote dates to a 2008 write-up of Omniture's practice. Variants and common slips are in our [SaaS magic number guide](/startup/saas-magic-number).

## Capital efficiency: burn, runway, burn multiple and Rule of 40

### Burn and runway

**Monthly net burn** is operating cash out minus operating cash in for the month; money raised and loans don't count as inflows. **Runway (months) = cash balance ÷ monthly net burn.** $1.2M in the bank at $50,000 a month is 24 months. At $175,000 a month it is 6.9.

Both burn figures are High Alpha's 2026 medians: $50,000 a month under $5M ARR, $175,000 at $5M to $20M. Respondents gave burn as a range, scored at the midpoint, so read these as ranges. We did not find a dated runway benchmark with a named population, so we don't give one. We would compute runway twice: on current burn, and on the burn you plan after the next hires.

### Burn multiple

**Burn multiple = net burn ÷ net new ARR**, over the same period. David Sacks introduced it in [April 2020](https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-burn-multiple-51a7e43cb200). Lower is better. His tiers for venture-stage startups: under 1x "Amazing", 1x to 1.5x "Great", 1.5x to 2x "Good", 2x to 3x "Suspect", over 3x "Bad". He expected a seed company to sit near 3 and drop toward 2 after the Series A.

The same glossary that redefines the magic number gives the burn multiple as cash balance ÷ burn rate and calls a high number good. That formula is runway.

Benchmarkit's 2026 report puts the median at 1.0x (0.4x to 2.0x, n = 62): 1.0x under $5M ARR and 1.3x at $5M to $20M (chart-read). Our [burn multiple guide](/startup/burn-multiple) has more datasets by band.

### Rule of 40

**Rule of 40: YoY growth rate % + profit margin % ≥ 40%.** [Brad Feld wrote it up in February 2015](https://feld.com/archives/2015/02/rule-40-healthy-saas-company/), passing on a late-stage investor's rule for SaaS companies at scale, which he put at $50M or more in revenue; he prefers EBITDA as the profit measure. Benchmarkit's 2026 median is 25% for 2025 (7% to 43%, n = 110), without a stated profit measure, and the report says the rule "becomes more meaningful as companies approach ~$20M ARR and above." High Alpha's median across all its respondents is 33% (9% to 52%), on YoY ARR growth plus trailing 12-month free cash flow or EBITDA margin, and it notes that "In every ARR band above $1M ARR the upper quartile is above 40% and the median is below it." Under $1M, High Alpha's median is 50%. Our reading: that is mostly growth off a small base. Our [Rule of 40 guide](/startup/rule-of-40) covers the profit-measure choice.

## Run your numbers

Our [calculator](/calculator) takes MRR, total customers, monthly revenue churn (the share of MRR lost to cancellations and downgrades), CAC per customer and gross margin (prefilled at 80%), plus optional new and expansion MRR. It works out average revenue per account (ARPA) as MRR ÷ total customers, then returns four numbers:

- LTV = ARPA × gross margin ÷ monthly churn, so it is gross-profit based
- LTV:CAC = LTV ÷ CAC, labelled with our own bands, not a publisher's: 5 and above "Excellent", 3 to 4.99 "Good", 1 to 2.99 "Below target", below 1 "Needs improvement"
- Net MRR growth = (new and expansion MRR - MRR × churn) ÷ MRR
- CAC payback = CAC ÷ (ARPA × gross margin), in months

Its payback uses average revenue across all customers rather than new customers, so treat it as an estimate if new customers pay noticeably more or less than your base. Everything else on this page (YoY ARR growth, GRR, NRR, the magic number, the burn multiple, the Rule of 40, runway) needs the formulas above.

## FAQ

### Which SaaS metrics matter at seed?

No primary source we found maps seed rounds to an ARR level, so think in ARR. Under $1M ARR, track MRR and its growth, gross margin, customers lost each month, burn and runway. CAC payback is understated if founder selling time isn't allocated, as High Alpha warns, and GRR and NRR need cohorts that have reached a renewal.

### What do investors look at first?

No survey measures it; two named investor views exist. Skok calls months to recover CAC "one of the four key metrics", with growth of net new ARR, burn rate and net revenue retention. Craft Ventures' 2021 Series A benchmarks, published by David Sacks, set a growth bar: compound monthly growth of at least 15% below $1M ARR and 10% above. Both are opinions tied to a firm and a year.

### What is the 3-3-2-2-2 (T2D3) rule?

It is Neeraj Agrawal's 2015 growth path from Battery Ventures: after product-market fit and $2M ARR, triple twice (to $6M, then $18M), then double three times ($36M, $72M, $144M). The first tripling is 200% growth, above High Alpha's 2026 75th percentile for $1M to $5M (145%). Fewer than one in four of High Alpha's respondents in that band grow that fast.

### Should usage-based revenue count as ARR?

It can, if you say how. Usage revenue moves with consumption, so one month annualized can mislead. Our reading: annualize a trailing average, keep committed subscription revenue visible on its own, and label the method. Both surveys that split by pricing show higher NRR for usage: 108% against 98% for seat-based in Benchmarkit 2026, and 112% against 100% for subscription, seat-based and hybrid companies in High Alpha 2026.

### What is a good growth rate for a SaaS startup?

It depends on your ARR and the survey. Under $1M ARR, High Alpha's 2026 median is 100% and SaaS Capital's 2026 chart reads about 40%. At $1M to $5M, High Alpha's median is 51%. Across all sizes, Benchmarkit's 2026 median is 20% and SaaS Capital's 22%. Name the survey beside your number.
